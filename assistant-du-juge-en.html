Judge’s Assistant

Understand, question and improve the way you judge

As a practical extension of the book Le jugement en danse, the Judge’s Assistant helps judges, future judges, trainers, dancers and coaches reflect on real judgment situations: perception, attention, emotions, cognitive biases, intuition, comparison, fatigue and personal development.

The book explains the human mechanisms involved in judgment. The Judge’s Assistant helps apply them to a concrete situation: a doubt, a difficulty encountered in competition, a training question, a debriefing or a personal area for improvement.

Why this assistant?

Judging is not only about applying a grid or producing a ranking. A judge observes, selects, compares, feels, sometimes doubts, and then decides in a real context, with constraints of attention, perception, emotion, experience and time.

The Judge’s Assistant has been designed to help clarify what happens during this process. It does not replace official rules, institutional training or field experience. It offers a space for reflection, clarification and progress.

Who is it for?

The assistant may be useful for several audiences:

  • judges who wish to analyse and improve their practice;
  • future judges who want to understand the mechanisms of judgment;
  • trainers who wish to create exercises or practical situations;
  • dancers and coaches who want to better understand the logic of judgment;
  • anyone interested in cognitive biases, attention, perception and decision-making.

What the assistant can do

  • explain a concept related to judgment: attention, perception, emotion, cognitive bias, intuition;
  • analyse a situation experienced by a judge, dancer, coach or trainer;
  • suggest a self-positioning grid or a short questionnaire;
  • help identify a personal point of vigilance;
  • prepare a debriefing after a competition;
  • build a small personal protocol for progress;
  • prepare a training exercise for beginner or experienced judges.

What the assistant does not do

The Judge’s Assistant remains deliberately limited to its field.

  • It does not say who should have won a competition.
  • It does not replace official rules or judging systems.
  • It does not settle disputes or appeals.
  • It does not accuse a judge, dancer, coach or institution.
  • It does not provide legal, tax, medical, social or administrative advice.
  • It does not answer as a general-purpose AI outside the field of judgment in dance.

Its role is to shed light on possible mechanisms, not to judge people.

Examples of questions you can ask

  • “I am a judge and I do not know where to start.”
  • “I think I may have been influenced by a very visible couple.”
  • “Why do competent judges not always see the same thing?”
  • “I would like to know which area I should work on as a judge.”
  • “Prepare an exercise to train beginner judges on limited attention.”
  • “When I judge, I often take previous rankings into account.”
  • “I am a dancer and I do not understand why my result differs from what I felt.”
