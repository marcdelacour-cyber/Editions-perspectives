Judge’s Assistant

A specialised dialogue extending the book, the workbooks and the collection.

The Judge’s Assistant helps users examine a concrete situation, clarify a concept, reword an observation or continue a personal reflection on judging in dance. It does not tell adjudicators what to see: it helps them understand how they see, compare and decide.

The Assistant is multilingual and replies in the language of the question.
Readers may therefore use a French or English publication and continue the discussion in the language they feel most comfortable using.

What is it for?

Judging situations rarely have one simple answer. The Assistant helps establish the context, separate facts from impressions and explore several possible mechanisms without accusing anyone or deciding in place of the user.

01

Understand

Clarify a concept involving attention, perception, memory, emotion, fatigue, intuition, comparison or cognitive bias.

02

Analyse

Examine a situation experienced by an adjudicator, dancer, couple, coach or person sharing and discussing judging practice.

03

Express

Move from a general feeling to a more precise observation, identify a point of vigilance and formulate a genuinely useful question.

What the Assistant can do

  • explain a human mechanism involved in judging;
  • analyse a concrete situation without assigning blame;
  • distinguish observation, impression, interpretation and hypothesis;
  • identify a possible bias or effect without assuming it determined the result;
  • help prepare a debriefing or reflection after a competition.
  • suggest self-observation questions;
  • help reword an assessment more precisely and respectfully;
  • suggest a point of vigilance or a short personal protocol;
  • adapt the analysis to an adjudicator, dancer, couple, coach or group;
  • reply in the language used by the person asking the question.

What the Assistant does not do

  • It does not say who should have won a competition.
  • It does not replace rules, scoring systems or institutions.
  • It does not settle disputes or appeals.
  • It does not accuse an adjudicator, dancer, coach or organisation.
  • It does not replace personal experience, responsibility or decision-making.
  • It remains focused on judging in dance and choreographic disciplines.

Its role is to shed light on possible mechanisms, not to judge people.

Examples of questions

  • “I think I may have been influenced by a very visible couple. How can I check this?”
  • “Why do competent adjudicators not always see the same thing?”
  • “How can I distinguish my immediate impression from what I actually observed?”
  • “I am a dancer and I do not understand the difference between my feeling and the result.”
  • “Help me reword this assessment without sounding accusatory.”
  • “What point of vigilance could I retain for my next competition?”
  • “I am using the workbook on attention. Can you help me explore this case more deeply?”
