Judging and understanding judgement

Judging in Dance

Understanding what really happens when we observe, compare and decide

What if judging better began with understanding what our attention selects… and what it leaves out?

Printed book — €24
Understanding judgement is not about imposing one way of judging. It is about recognising more clearly what can influence our attention and our decisions.

You may already have asked yourself one of these questions

Two competent adjudicators can watch the same performance without reading it in exactly the same way. The book begins with that reality and explores what happens between what we see and the decision we make.

Why can two fair-minded adjudicators rank the same couple differently?
Did I watch every couple with the same degree of availability?
Did a highly visible couple occupy more of my attention than it should have?
Do I remember precise observations… or mainly the impression the couple created?
Did fatigue, running order or context alter the way I looked?
How can I distinguish what attracts me spontaneously from what genuinely supports my judgement?

Judgement is not a simple photograph of the performance

Observing, comparing and deciding involve human mechanisms. Understanding them does not diminish expertise; it helps us see more clearly what expertise relies on and where our own points of vigilance may lie.

Understand what captures attention

Selective attention, salience, immediate memory, experience, preferences, emotion and fatigue can influence what we notice, what we retain and what we compare.

Make the decision process more explicit

The aim is not to remove intuition, but to distinguish more clearly between impression, criteria, comparison and decision so that we can revisit our own practice with greater awareness.

What you will explore

The book brings together cognitive psychology, field situations and judging experience to put words to mechanisms that are often experienced without being clearly identified.

Attention and perceptionWhat attracts the eye, what remains less visible, and how selective observation shapes what becomes available for judgement.
Memory and comparisonWhat we retain from a performance and how impressions are formed, recalled and compared.
Emotion, fatigue and contextTheir possible influence on the adjudicator’s availability, attention and decision-making.
Preferences and cognitive biasesReputation, appearance, halo effects, salience and other mechanisms that may orient attention.
Intuition, analysis and expertiseThe role of experience, fast reasoning and more analytical forms of control.
The panel and the perception of resultsHow panels work and how dancers, coaches and audiences interpret differences between judgements.

No perfect neutrality — but greater awareness

The book does not try to turn adjudicators into machines or remove every difference in appreciation. It invites readers to identify possible influences on attention, distinguish observed information from the impression produced, and build their own points of vigilance.

Understanding more clearly what influences our attention helps us take greater responsibility for the decisions we make.

Two complementary perspectives

The book is informed both by a scientific perspective on the mechanisms of judgement and by experience at the highest level of dance.

The book continues with you: Judging Assistant included

Once the book has been read, some questions only arise from your own experience. The Judging Assistant lets you explore a situation you have experienced, clarify a concept or continue a line of reflection grounded in the book.

“I think I may have been influenced by a very visible couple. How can I analyse what happened?”
“Two adjudicators ranked this couple very differently. What might explain the difference without immediately assuming that one of them was wrong?”
“After a competition, how can I revisit my judgement without convincing myself afterwards that I must have been right?”
“I feel naturally drawn to certain styles of dancing. How can I make that a point of vigilance without pretending I have no preferences?”
Deliberately shortened example

Question: “I think I may have been influenced by a very visible couple. How can I avoid that at my next competition?”

The Assistant first distinguishes what attracted your attention from what actually supported your decision. It then asks you to step back: what did you really observe? Did you watch this couple for longer? Do you remember specific criteria, or mainly an overall impression?

The full example goes further: it explains a possible mechanism, helps separate “visible” from “better”, then suggests points of vigilance to adapt to your own way of judging.

Who is this book for?

Adjudicators and panel chairs

To question their own attention, explain their choices more clearly and recognise the constraints within which judgement is formed.
