Mental preparation and competition

When the Unexpected Takes the Floor

Mental Preparation for Dance Competitions

You step onto the floor with your choreography and your certainties. The unexpected steps onto the floor too.

Printed book — €30
You cannot make competition entirely predictable. You can learn to recognise what is happening and train ways of responding when the real situation no longer matches the plan.

You may recognise yourself in one of these situations

The competition you actually face is never exactly the one you prepared for. Sometimes a few seconds are enough to unsettle reference points that felt completely secure in training.

I dance better in training than I do in competition.
After one mistake, I try to make up for it… and things get worse.
I lose the music or the choreography and no longer know what to do.
My partner does not react as expected and we become disorganised.
The judges, the waiting or the result take up too much mental space.
I cannot leave one bad dance behind before the next one begins.

The issue is not simply to “manage stress”

Understand what has changed

A mistake, an unfamiliar floor, music you would not have chosen, another couple, a loss of connection or an intrusive thought can alter attention, reference points and communication.

Prepare responses rather than a perfect scenario

The aim is not to make competition predictable. It is to widen your range of possible actions when reality no longer resembles the initial plan.

What you will work on

The book begins with concrete situations and explores several approaches drawn from mental preparation. It does not propose one universal method: it helps readers test, combine and build their own responses.

Build your resourcesGoals, routines, a toolbox, communication and preparation for adaptation.
When the dance goes off courseMistakes, memory, choreography, loss of reference points and the relationship with a partner.
When the environment forces you to adaptThe floor, other couples, trajectories, music and the capacity to change.
Attention, thoughts and being watchedJudges, audiences, comparison, inner dialogue and returning to what is happening now.
Emotions, arousal and recoveryToo much or too little arousal, breathing, refocusing and the period between dances.
Support, results and feedbackChoosing the support you need, putting a placing in perspective and making feedback useful.
Build your personal protocolTest, observe, combine, practise and adjust what actually works for you.

No miracle recipe. No universal profile.

Two dancers facing an apparently similar situation may need different responses. Research and tools are used here to illuminate mechanisms and open possible paths — not to turn field examples into rules that apply to everyone.

“Here is what you may be able to observe. Here are several possibilities. Now test them and discover what actually helps you.”

The book continues with you: Competitor Assistant included

A personal situation does not always fit neatly into a chapter. The Competitor Assistant extends the book: you describe what happened, and it helps you turn the book’s principles into lines of enquiry adapted to your situation. It is not intended to be consulted in the few seconds between two dances. Its value lies mainly in preparing beforehand or analysing afterwards, so that a response can be understood, tested and trained before it is needed.

“At my last competition I made a mistake in the waltz and carried it into the tango. How can I understand what happened and prepare for next time?”
“I lose the music after a small mistake. How can I work out what triggers that loss of reference points for me?”
“My partner wants to talk while we are waiting, but I need silence. How can we find a way of functioning that suits both of us?”
“In the 15 minutes before I dance, how can I identify what really helps me instead of copying somebody else’s routine?”
Fictitious and deliberately shortened example — “I made a mistake in the waltz and carried it into the tango. How can I prepare for next time?”

The Assistant would not begin by imposing a routine. It would first try to understand what actually happened: what the dancer was thinking, what changed in the body or within the couple, what they tried, and what helped — or did not.

In the fuller example, three successive exchanges reveal that the dancer did not lose the music. Instead, they began trying to make up for the waltz, became tighter and carried that intention into the tango. They also explain that a prescribed breathing technique does not suit them, whereas a few seconds of silence and a simple bodily cue help more.

The possible response therefore becomes personal: learn to recognise the signal “I am trying to compensate”, test outside competition a return to a cue that genuinely suits this dancer, then observe whether it actually helps. Different answers from the dancer would have led to a different line of enquiry.

See the full fictitious 3-question conversation

The Assistant is multilingual and replies in the language used to ask the question. It does not impose a single answer and does not replace a coach or individual professional support. Its role is to observe, question, connect elements of a situation and explore possibilities to test. The more personal the need, the more personal the response should become.

Who is this book for?

Competitors

To understand what changes between training and competition and prepare responses when a familiar reference point disappears.

Couples

To identify more clearly what happens in connection, communication and recovery after a difficulty.

Coaches and teachers

To use situations, questions and tools that help a dancer without imposing one universal recipe.
