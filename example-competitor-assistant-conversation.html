A fictitious conversation in three questions

How the Assistant can start from one lived situation without turning a possible response into a universal recipe.

Fictitious example. This exchange was written to show the Assistant’s dialogue process. It is neither a model answer nor a routine to reproduce. Different answers from the dancer would lead to different hypotheses and different possibilities to test.

Starting situation: after a disappointing waltz, the dancer feels that the mistake followed them into the tango. They return to the situation later, after the competition, when there is time to analyse it calmly.

Dancer“At my last competition I made a mistake in the waltz and carried it into the tango. How can I prepare better for next time?”
AssistantBefore looking for a technique, I would like to understand what you mean by “carried it into the tango”. Between leaving the waltz and starting the tango, what was mainly occupying your attention? And what did you notice in your body or in the connection with your partner?
Dancer“I kept thinking about the mistake and telling myself that I had ruined the waltz. My partner was telling me to move on. I was tighter, and in the tango I tried to do more because I wanted to make up for it.”
AssistantThere is already a useful distinction here: you are not describing “stress” in general. You are describing a thought that remains attached to the previous dance, followed by an intention to compensate in the next one. When that intention appears, what is the first concrete sign you can recognise? For example: do you speed up, tighten your partner, try to control more, talk more, lose the music…? What happened in your case?
Dancer“I did not lose the music. But I held my partner more tightly and wanted to show that the tango would be better. I felt I had to recover the competition.”
AssistantYour first warning sign may therefore be less “I am stressed” than “I am starting to compensate”. That still does not tell us what you should do. We now need to find what genuinely helps you become available for the next dance again. When you recover well in training or after a difficulty, what tends to help most: a few seconds of silence, an exchange with your partner, movement, a bodily cue, a word, a particular way of breathing, or something else?
Dancer“I need silence more than anything. Breathing exercises make me feel as though I am adding another technique. But when I simply think about my weight into the floor and the connection with my partner, I restart better.”
AssistantThen it would make little sense to impose a breathing routine just because it works for somebody else. One possibility to test could be: learn to recognise the signal “I am trying to compensate”, allow yourself a few seconds of silence when circumstances permit, then return to one very simple bodily reference — here, weight into the floor and connection — before starting again. The important part is to practise this in several training situations and observe whether it actually makes you more available. If it does not, you adjust.

What if your answers had been different? If you had said that you lost the music, talked continuously, disconnected from your partner or became under-activated, the Assistant would have explored other hypotheses and other possibilities.

What this conversation illustrates

The response was not contained in the initial question. It emerged progressively from the dancer’s own observations.

The starting point is therefore not: “Which routine should I use?” but rather: “What actually happened for me, what signal can I learn to recognise, and what deserves to be tested?”

The book and the Assistant share the same logic: observe, understand, experiment, adjust. The cases are there to help build personal responses, not to memorise recipes.
